Lens or Prison? The Long Argument About Language and Thought

For almost a century, scientists have argued about whether the words we speak decide what we are able to think. The answer turns out to be more interesting than a simple yes or no.

AIn the 1930s, a chemical engineer named Benjamin Lee Whorf was working as a fire-prevention inspector for an insurance company in Connecticut. Studying languages was merely his hobby, yet one of his workplace observations would go on to shape an entire field. Whorf noticed that workers were far more careless around gasoline drums labelled "empty" than around full ones, even though the "empty" drums, still filled with explosive vapour, were arguably the more dangerous of the two. The word, he concluded, was doing the thinking for them.

BBuilding on the work of his mentor, the anthropologist Edward Sapir, Whorf went on to posit something far more ambitious: that the grammar of a language shapes the way its speakers perceive reality. In its strongest form, the idea, later known as the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, implies that language is a kind of prison. If your language lacks a word for something, you are simply unable to think about it. Whorf's most famous example was the Hopi people of Arizona, whose language, he claimed, contained no words or grammatical forms referring to time.

CIt was a compelling story, but a tenuous one. In 1983 the linguist Ekkehart Malotki published a study several hundred pages long, packed with Hopi expressions for days, seasons, the past and the future. The strong version of the hypothesis was not alone in its downfall. The popular claim that the Inuit have hundreds of words for snow turned out to have grown with every retelling, from a handful of examples in an early anthropological text to ever larger and more far-fetched numbers. By the 1990s, many linguists regarded linguistic relativity as thoroughly debunked.

DAnd yet the idea refused to die. In a 2007 experiment, researchers showed Russian and English speakers three squares of blue and asked them to pick which of the bottom two matched the top one. Russian has no single everyday word for "blue": it divides the range into goluboy (lighter blues) and siniy (darker blues). When the two shades fell on different sides of that boundary, Russian speakers answered measurably faster, while English speakers showed no such advantage. Strikingly, the effect vanished when participants had to silently rehearse a string of digits at the same time, which suggests that language was actively involved in the act of seeing.

Language seems to act less like a prison than like a lens.

EOther findings are equally counterintuitive. Speakers of Kuuk Thaayorre, an Aboriginal language spoken in Pormpuraaw on the Cape York Peninsula in Australia, rarely use words like "left" and "right" to locate things. Instead, they rely on cardinal directions: a cup sits to the north-east of a plate, an ant crawls on your south-west leg. When asked to arrange cards showing a sequence of events, such as a man growing older, they tended to lay them out from east to west, whichever way they happened to be facing. Meanwhile, members of the Pirahã community in the Amazon, whose language reportedly lacks words for exact numbers, could line up objects to match a set placed in front of them, but struggled once they had to remember an exact quantity.

FNot every result has held up, however. A widely reported study suggested that German speakers, for whom the word for "bridge" is grammatically feminine, tend to describe bridges as "elegant", while Spanish speakers, for whom it is masculine, call them "strong". Later attempts to replicate such findings have produced mixed results, a reminder that a striking headline is not the same thing as robust evidence.

GSo where does this leave us? Few researchers today believe that language constrains what we are able to think; the strong version is effectively dead. But a more nuanced, weaker version is alive and well. Language seems to act less like a prison than like a lens, subtly directing our attention to some features of the world rather than others. Whorf's gasoline drums may have been the best illustration all along. The workers were perfectly capable of understanding that vapour burns. Their language simply made it easy not to notice.
